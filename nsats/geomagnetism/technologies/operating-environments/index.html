Underground, on the ground and in the air

NSATS identifies underground, surface, airborne, underwater, indoor and dense urban operation as intended application environments for geomagnetic positioning. The equipment and the task in each setting define what a useful implementation needs to provide.

An intended application environment names a setting for which a licensee is expected to design its implementation; it describes the use being considered rather than a location at which results have been reported.

Underground

Underground use is an intended direction. A mine or tunnel application may need to locate inspection equipment, mobile machinery or a personnel device. The route, surrounding infrastructure and movement of equipment help define the assessment conditions. A useful study would relate position information to the actual task, such as locating an observation or identifying where a device is situated.

An underground route is more than its start and destination. It can include travel between working areas, stops to inspect equipment and returns through places already visited. Locating a stationary work point and following equipment between work areas place different demands on the application’s use of position.

On the ground

Surface use is an intended direction, including industrial sites, transport routes, indoor spaces, dense urban areas and mobile equipment. Requirements vary between an asset being located occasionally and a vehicle requiring frequent position updates. The operating area, surrounding structures and expected movement should shape both the integration and the evaluation of a geomagnetic position input.

A position update is a newly supplied estimate of location. A moving platform needs repeated updates if its application is to follow the movement, because the place associated with an earlier output may no longer describe its current position. An asset remaining at one place may instead need a location when a particular task calls for it.

A surface task can alternate between movement and stationary work. In a general positioning assessment, the observation schedule should follow those stages. Otherwise a study of stationary equipment may say little about the part of the task performed while moving.

In the air

Airborne use is an intended direction. An airborne application needs to consider the platform, motion and intended use of position information. An aerial inspection task may have different requirements from another aircraft system. Evaluation should define the flight conditions and the relationship between the positioning implementation and the aircraft, then assess the information against the application’s requirements.

An airborne platform can change both its position and its orientation during work. The equipment it carries may also have its own mounting and movement. The movement during collection can differ from the movement during travel to or from the inspection area.

Local magnetic surroundings

Magnetic measurements can include contributions from nearby sources as well as Earth’s main field. Iron objects and electrical installations can affect the local magnetic environment. This explains why the surroundings of a device matter when studying a magnetic measurement: a route through equipment or infrastructure presents conditions beyond its geographical location alone.

Earth’s main magnetic field is the planet-scale field originating in its interior and changing slowly over time.

In ordinary magnetic measurement, a nearby object and an electrical current can contribute to the field observed at the measurement point. The surroundings can change when equipment moves or electrical activity changes. Recording the equipment state and nearby activity distinguishes an observation made in one local setting from another made at the same geographical place under different conditions. The observation record can distinguish fixed structures from objects that move during the task.
