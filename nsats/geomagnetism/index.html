NSATS Geomagnetism

NSATS has developed technology that uses Earth’s magnetic field to determine location without relying on live GPS satellite signals. This independence provides resilience against GPS spoofing, which uses false signals to mislead a receiver, and GPS jamming, which disrupts reception of satellite signals. The technology is available under licence for incorporation into chips, embedded hardware, devices and systems. Intended applications include positioning underground, on the surface, in the air, underwater, indoors and in dense urban areas.

Location from Earth’s magnetic field

NSATS’s geomagnetic positioning technology determines geographic coordinates—latitude and longitude—using Earth’s magnetic field, without relying on live GPS satellite signals. It provides the type of location information normally obtained from GPS, identifying where equipment is rather than simply which direction it faces.

Application software can use these coordinates to display equipment’s location on a map or retain its position alongside a record of activity. This allows an operator to see where equipment is and review where its work took place. Where the position needs to reach a remote operator, the surrounding system provides the communications needed to transmit it.

Underground

Mines, tunnels and enclosed infrastructure create a practical need to locate equipment and personnel where live GPS satellite signals may be unavailable. Underground positioning is an intended application of NSATS’s geomagnetic technology, which can be incorporated into inspection devices, mobile equipment and devices carried by personnel.

In an illustrative tunnel inspection, a device could record its geographic coordinates when collecting an observation. The inspection software would associate that position with the activity performed, allowing an operator to distinguish a place that was inspected from one the device merely passed through. The coordinates locate the device; the inspection record establishes what was examined and what was found. An implementation intended to distinguish particular tunnel sections or inspection points would need to be evaluated against that requirement in the underground environment.

On the ground

Surface vehicles, mobile robots and industrial assets are intended applications of NSATS’s geomagnetic positioning technology, including equipment operating indoors and in dense urban areas. Position information can help an operator locate equipment across a site or enable an inspection application to associate observations with positions along a route.

An illustrative warehouse application could show a mobile vehicle’s position on a site plan alongside its operating status. The coordinates would establish where the vehicle was; information from its controls or task software would indicate whether it was travelling, carrying out a task or waiting. This distinction matters because a stationary vehicle may be working or idle.

In the air

Aircraft and aerial inspection drones are intended applications of NSATS’s geomagnetic positioning technology. Geographic coordinates derived from Earth’s magnetic field can supply an independent source of position information to navigation equipment, reducing reliance on live GPS satellite signals.

An onboard system could use those coordinates to establish where the aircraft is along a planned route. Heading, height and movement provide additional information for navigation and flight control; they remain distinct from geographic position. For aerial inspection, application software can associate observations with the aircraft’s position when they were collected, while separately recording what was inspected and presenting the findings to an operator.

Evaluation must establish whether the position information meets the requirements of the equipment receiving it and the task being performed. This includes assessing the implementation in its intended aircraft, taking account of the installation, aircraft motion and operating environment.

Implementation under licence

Implementation begins with a defined use for the position information: what needs to be located, which person or system will use the coordinates and what action they support. These requirements guide where the licensed positioning function is incorporated—in a chip, embedded hardware, a device or a wider hardware and software system—and how it connects to the application.

The licensee specifies the interfaces through which the application receives coordinates, including their format and the frequency of updates required for the task. Application software must then make that information usable: presenting a location clearly to an operator or retaining it with the activity it describes so that it can be retrieved later. The equipment designer uses these requirements to identify the components, software functions and connections needed to incorporate positioning into the intended solution.

Positioning resilience

NSATS’s geomagnetic technology provides an alternative source of geographic coordinates when GPS signals are disrupted or misleading. It determines location from Earth’s magnetic field, avoiding dependence on the satellite signals targeted by GPS jamming and spoofing.

This independence also applies to signal replay attacks, known as meaconing, in which genuine satellite signals are rebroadcast to mislead a receiver. The geomagnetic position calculation does not depend on those signals. It also avoids reliance on GPS broadcasts when attacks on satellites or ground infrastructure disrupt the service.

For this alternative source to support continued operation, the application must be designed to use it. A system combining GPS and geomagnetic coordinates needs a defined response when the two disagree, and when no available position meets the task’s requirements. Its software, position records and communications require their own security measures. Evaluation must establish how the complete implementation performs in its intended operating environment.

Location in an operational task

During a mobile task, the equipment’s current position and its position when an activity took place serve different purposes. An operator may need to see where the equipment is now, while someone reviewing the work later needs the location associated with the recorded activity.

In an illustrative inspection, a device captures its coordinates with a finding and then continues along its route. The operator’s display follows its changing position, while the inspection record preserves the coordinates from the observation for later review. That record also identifies what was inspected: the device’s coordinates alone do not identify an object observed from a distance.
