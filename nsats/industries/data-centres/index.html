Data centres

NSATS’s Quantum technology enables manufacturers of data centre equipment to increase effective storage and working-memory capacity and support faster processing and data transfer. For operators, these capabilities can accommodate a larger workload within the available equipment or reduce the equipment needed for an established workload. Their contribution depends on the information being handled, the demands of the service and the complete hardware implementation.

Existing drives and RAM incorporate the technology through an added, specially designed chipset or controller. Drives continue to retain information for later use, while RAM holds the working data needed by running programs. A separate implementation route combines retained storage and working memory in a purpose-designed component. These routes require hardware integration, with the connections and supporting resources suited to the equipment’s intended work.

Storage and working-memory capacity

NSATS’s Temporal Compression technology enables storage manufacturers to incorporate specialised gates, connectors and circuits that allow more original information to be retained within a given physical storage resource. The increase is in effective capacity; the physical capacity of the storage medium remains unchanged. The achievable result depends on the data, the device and the implementation, including all information needed to retrieve the original data.

Working memory addresses a different requirement. Increased effective RAM capacity can accommodate a larger active dataset or reduce the installed memory needed by a defined workload. That requirement includes inputs, temporary calculation space and results being prepared, together with the overlapping demands of programs running at the same time.

Where effective capacity increases through processing, its growth rate and upper limit describe how much additional information the implementation can accommodate over time. They do not establish how quickly new data can be accepted or a stored record returned. Capacity planning must therefore account for simultaneous storage, retrieval and processing, including the peak demand placed on the system.

Processing and data transfer

NSATS’s specialised gates support faster calculations, while its memory and internal connections supply the information those calculations need. The quantum-classical bus carries electrical signals with more than two states, beyond binary 0 and 1, supporting faster exchanges between processors, memory and other components. The effect on a service depends on how much of its work involves those operations and whether the surrounding system can supply and receive data at the required rate.

Transfers between sites use a separate networking arrangement. Compatible quantum-classical network cards at both endpoints process information for transmission and receipt, with ordinary routers carrying the traffic between them. For replication, backup or a scheduled data exchange, the relevant result is the time until the receiving application can use the complete information, including any encoding, decoding and encryption processing.

Equipment investment and expansion

Greater effective capacity can reduce the number of drives or memory modules required for a planned workload. Where this removes equipment from the complete configuration, it can also reduce demand for rack space, power connections and supporting infrastructure. The useful gain depends on the facility’s limiting resource: equipment budget, available space, electrical capacity or cooling provision.

The investment comparison must include the added chipsets or controllers, licensing, integration and data migration. A smaller stored dataset does not necessarily eliminate a drive, and a capacity improvement does not automatically remove a rack or cooling requirement. Savings arise where the proposed configuration reduces actual purchasing or operating requirements while meeting the same service needs.

New facilities can incorporate the complete arrangement into their equipment and infrastructure plans. Existing facilities can consider it during expansion or a scheduled refresh, identifying which equipment will remain in service and which will be replaced. Each planned addition needs its own comparison using the expected workload, equipment prices and operating requirements.

Electricity, cooling and maintenance

NSATS’s chipsets operate with lower heat than conventional computing, and the technology is designed for lower energy use. The effect on a data centre depends on the complete configuration, including the added controllers, memory, connections and other equipment supporting the workload. Lower component heat reduces facility cooling demand only where it lowers the heat that the cooling system must remove.

A configuration with fewer devices may also require less monitoring, servicing and replacement. These operating benefits should be assessed over the same service period as the equipment investment, using the maintenance requirements and expected replacement needs of the proposed configuration.

Applications and benefits

These potential uses depend on the data, workload and complete implementation.

  • Lower equipment costs for storage expansion
  • Larger working datasets for hosted applications
  • More retained data within existing racks
  • Faster exchanges between processors and memory
  • Faster replication between equipped data centres
  • Reduced equipment requirements for archival storage

Integration and service continuity

Implementation begins with the retained data, peak working-memory demand and processing and transfer requirements of the intended service. The equipment design must support those activities together, including periods when new information arrives while existing records are being retrieved. The project must also identify which interfaces can change and which parts of the facility will remain in use.

Migration planning needs to preserve data integrity and continuity of service. Backup, replication and recovery arrangements remain part of the operator’s system and must work with the new equipment. These requirements belong in the integration and acceptance plan alongside normal performance, interruptions and recovery.

Operators that design their own storage or server equipment can discuss the relevant technology directly with NSATS. Operators that purchase equipment can raise their capacity and performance requirements with the manufacturers responsible for its design.

Comparing complete configurations

Use the same workload, service requirements and planning period for both configurations. The following measures connect the technology’s contribution to the facility’s investment and operating decisions.

AreaWhat to compare
Total costEquipment, added controllers, licensing, integration, migration, maintenance and continuing operation.
CapacityEffective retained-storage capacity and working-memory capacity, reported separately, including supporting information and resources.
Capacity growth and accessThe rate and upper limit of effective-capacity growth, alongside the rates of accepting and retrieving data and the delay in returning a requested record.
Completed workTime from a request to a usable result, completed workload and transfer performance, including processing at both endpoints.
Energy and coolingEnergy for equivalent work, whole-facility electricity use, heat output and cooling demand, including the added equipment.
Space and maintenanceDrives, memory modules, racks and supporting equipment required, together with servicing and replacement needs.

Environmental considerations

Reducing the equipment required for a workload can reduce the material and manufacturing demands associated with capacity expansion. The comparison must also include the added hardware, equipment retained in service, replacements and eventual disposal.

The carbon effect depends on electricity consumption, the electricity supply and the manufacture and service life of the equipment. It therefore needs to be established for the proposed installation using operating records and lifecycle information, rather than inferred from a component’s capacity or heat output alone.

Discussing an implementation

For an initial discussion, describe the workload, the information retained, the peak working-memory requirement and the facility constraint driving the investment. Include the planning period, equipment expected to remain in service and the availability and recovery requirements during migration. These details help identify the relevant functions and the integration work needed for the proposed configuration.
