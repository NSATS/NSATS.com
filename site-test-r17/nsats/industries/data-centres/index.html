Data centres

Expanding a data centre’s storage and working memory is a decision about equipment, electricity, cooling and space as well as capacity. NSATS licences enable classical devices to perform operations based on quantum mechanics, at room temperature. The quantum-classical bus carries electrical signals with more than two states, beyond binary 0 and 1, enabling faster processing, data transfer and decision making within a quantum-classical electronic device. Equipment built under licence holds the same data on fewer drives and less memory, sends and retrieves it faster, and uses chipsets that run with lower heat than conventional computing. Operators that design their own storage or server equipment can license the technology; others can raise it with the manufacturers that build their equipment.

Where the cost of growth sits

A capacity plan includes drives and memory, the equipment that connects and powers them, and the space and cooling needed to operate them. Maintenance and replacement continue throughout their service life. The constraint that determines the next investment differs between facilities: available power, cooling provision, usable space or the equipment budget.

Effective capacity matters because it changes how much data the installed storage and memory can hold. The relevant comparison is between complete configurations delivering the same workload and service level over the same planning period. That gives procurement, operations and finance a common basis for assessing an equipment change.

What the licensed technology changes

NSATS technology enables fewer drives to hold the same data through a specially designed classical chipset or controller that the licensee adds. The drives and RAM remain ordinary classical storage; integration is part of the licensee’s design, not a software-only upgrade or a drop-in change. The increase in effective capacity depends on the drive, how the technology is used and where the licensed functions sit in the design.

Classical data is encoded using quantum-mechanical logic, and reversible computing contributes to compression. The drive’s physical size stays unchanged while its effective storage capacity increases. For working memory, the technology enables the same working data to be held in less installed RAM.

Computer buses send and retrieve encoded classical data faster, and existing memory processes existing binary data faster. These contributions address the time spent supplying data to processing and moving results between components.

The chipsets run with lower heat than conventional computing, and the technology enables lower energy use. It operates at room temperature, without super-cooling. The effect on the facility’s electricity and cooling demand depends on the complete configuration, including the added controller’s power and heat, under the intended workload.

NSATS sells technology licences, not chips. Its technology helps licensees manufacture their own chips.

Capital expenditure

A planned data requirement needs fewer drives and memory modules, so less storage equipment has to be procured. Compare the complete investment, including controllers or chipsets, the licence, integration and migration, against equipment delivering the same workload and service level.

A new facility can plan its storage layout around the complete configuration, including the added controllers. A refresh programme provides a point at which to assess the change alongside already planned replacement work. The purchasing decision should identify equipment that will remain in service and equipment that will be replaced, so the comparison reflects the actual project.

Where the complete configuration uses less space or power for the same data, the facility can hold more data before reaching that particular limit. The useful gain depends on which constraint determines expansion. Assess deployment density and available cooling alongside the storage layout when deciding how much additional capacity fits.

Operating expenditure

Fewer drives and memory modules, chipsets with lower heat and lower energy use reduce the electricity, cooling and maintenance that the storage equipment needs for the same workload. Judge the case on total cost for a defined workload and service level, including the controller, licence, integration, migration and continuing operation.

Where the complete configuration, including added controllers, contains fewer devices to monitor, service and replace, it reduces the equipment maintenance workload. The maintenance comparison should use the service requirements and replacement needs of that configuration over the planning period.

Lower chipset heat contributes to lower cooling demand where it reduces the complete system’s heat output under comparable work. Its effect on facility cooling depends on how much of the heat load the changed equipment represents and how the cooling system responds. Include the new equipment’s own power and cooling requirements when assessing the result.

Each planned capacity addition is another point at which fewer drives and less memory hold the same data, so repeat the comparison for every planned addition, using the prices and operating requirements that apply to it.

Performance under your workload

Effective storage capacity, installed RAM, access time and completed work answer different questions. Capacity determines how much information can be held; faster sending and retrieval affect how quickly processing receives the data it needs. The benefit to completed work follows the share of the workload spent storing, retrieving and moving data.

Measure the task from the request to a usable result, including the host and the added equipment. Record the working-memory requirement and the access pattern alongside throughput and response time. This connects a component-level improvement to the work the facility must deliver.

For transfers between sites, assess the complete exchange through the equipped endpoints and the network between them. The Routers and networking section explains the separate network arrangement.

Capacity planning by rate

Effective capacity depends on processing time, not only physical size. A temporal drive is better described by how much data it can process and store per second than by a fixed capacity.

Capacity comparisons should therefore be made at the store-and-retrieve rate the facility actually needs. Include incoming data, requests for retained data and work that runs at the same time. The capacity plan then describes the effective storage available for the intended service, alongside the physical resources it occupies.

Environmental contribution

Lower energy use and cooler-running chipsets reduce the electricity and cooling needed for comparable useful work. Report the effect for the complete configuration, including the added controllers, together with the facility’s total electricity use.

Fewer drives and memory modules for the same data mean less storage equipment to manufacture and later retire. Include the added controllers, replacement, reuse and disposal when comparing complete configurations.

The carbon effect depends on the facility’s electricity supply and on the manufacture, service life and disposal of the equipment. Assess it from the operator’s energy records and lifecycle information for the selected configuration.

What implementation involves

The clearest starting points are storage- and memory-heavy facilities planning growth, a new installation or an equipment refresh. Define the retained data, peak working-memory requirement, retrieval demand and service levels that the equipment must support. The project also needs a clear boundary between the equipment being changed and the infrastructure that will remain.

Plan the migration of existing data and continuity of service during the change. Backup, replication, recovery and data-integrity arrangements remain part of the operator’s system design and should be evaluated with the new equipment. These requirements belong in the implementation plan alongside integration, acceptance and continuing operation.

What to compare

Use the same workload, service level and planning period for both configurations. The measures should connect the technical contribution to the investment decision:

  • Total cost, including equipment, licence, integration, migration and continuing operation.
  • Effective capacity per drive, rack and kilowatt at the required store-and-retrieve rate, with installed RAM reported separately.
  • Energy per useful task or stored-and-retrieved dataset, including the added equipment.
  • Whole-facility energy alongside IT energy.
  • Completed work, transfer throughput, access time and response time from request to usable result.
  • Heat output, cooling demand and maintenance workload for the complete configuration.

Use these measures to establish whether the configuration meets the facility’s capacity, performance and operating requirements within its budget and physical constraints.

Who builds it and how to proceed

Operators that design their own storage or server equipment can license the technology directly. Operators that purchase equipment can raise the required capacity and performance with the manufacturers that build it. The Memory and storage section identifies the functions manufacturers incorporate into their designs.

For a licensing discussion, bring the workload, stored and working-data requirements, the facility constraint that determines the next investment and the planning horizon. Include the equipment expected to remain and the service requirements during migration.
